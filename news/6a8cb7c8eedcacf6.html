Text settings Story text Size Small Standard Large Width * Standard Wide Links Standard Orange * Subscribers only Learn more Minimize to nav Lenders backing SpaceX’s latest $43 billion debt package, which is secured by its advanced AI chips, have tried to bar Elon Musk from firing the collateral into space as part of his plan for orbital data centers.

The world’s richest man has outlined ambitious plans to power AI models with data centers cooled by the vacuum of space. That will require packing satellites full of advanced chips.

SpaceX has said it plans to use the chips involved in the debt deal on Earth. But lenders have expressed concerns that if the group later decided to shoot the valuable chips that underpin their loans into space, it would prevent them from reclaiming and selling the semiconductors in the event of a default, according to people familiar with the matter.

It is uncertain whether the provision was included in the finalized documents.

The bonds were issued by a special purpose vehicle designed to keep the debt off SpaceX’s balance sheet and preserve its investment-grade credit rating.

The proposed debt covenant presented potential legal challenges for SpaceX’s ambitions to build data centers in space, highlighting the uncharted territory that investors need to navigate when financing the nascent asset class.

SpaceX sold more than $30 billion of the debt to large investors on Thursday as part of a financing package dubbed “Project Saturn.” The largest portion of the debt package was a $25 billion investment-grade bond backed by SpaceX’s five-year lease payment for the Nvidia chips.

The debt was sold at 3.45 percentage points above US Treasuries, indicating a total yield of about 8.3 percent, the people said. SpaceX’s corporate debt maturing in 2031, meanwhile, traded at a yield of about 7.2 percent on Thursday.

The newest bonds will mature in 2032, but the principal will be paid off in less than four years—an arrangement that was designed to reduce credit risks for lenders, the people added.

More than $19 billion of the bonds already secured commitments prior to Thursday, including $4 billion from Pimco.

Apollo and Goldman Sachs, which led the transaction, declined to comment. Pimco declined to comment. SpaceX did not respond to a request for comment.

Funds managed and advised by BlackRock, Goldman Sachs Asset Management, and KKR have also made anchor commitments in the transaction, according to SpaceX.

SpaceX plans to use the chips in US data centers majority-owned, controlled, and operated by SpaceX, according to a rating report issued by Moody’s.

Moody’s assigned the debt an A3 rating, one notch above SpaceX’s own credit rating. KBRA rated the debt A-minus.

The credit rating agencies attributed the A-tier rating in part to “hell-or-high-water” clauses that prevent SpaceX from walking away from the lease.

“The SpaceX GPU deal appears cleverly structured and should appeal to a panoply of buyers, potentially including deep pools of insurance capital seeking attractive yield on investment-grade debt,” said Grant Nachman, chief investment officer at Shorecliff Asset Management.

“This transaction could prove a harbinger of future issuances,” he said.

Additional reporting by Jill R Shah in New York.
