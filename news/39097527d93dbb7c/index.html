جنسن را برنگردان

منبع: Wired • ۲۹ آبان • ۱۹:۳۰ • ۸ دقیقه مطالعه
گروهی از برادران فناوری از انویدیا خواسته اند تا جنسن هوانگ را به عنوان مدیرعامل بازگرداند. ایده بدی است. Loader Save Story ذخیره این داستان Comment Loader Save Story ذخیره این داستان در اوایل تراژدی شکسپیر ژولیوس سزار، شخصیت عنوان به‌عنوان نماد قدرت مطلق به‌عنوان یک تا...

Loader Save Story Save this story Comment Loader Save Story Save this story Early in Shakespeare’s tragedy Julius Caesar , the title character is publicly offered a crown as a symbol of absolute power. Caesar refuses it three times, dramatically fainting after the final try. Scholars say that Caesar’s “falling sickness,” as his eventual assassin Brutus put it, masked his true intention to rule. His effort to win over the crowd with his humility only intensified the momentum to grant him more power. Indeed, after his refusal, his adherents became even more impassioned, setting the stage for his eventual claim to kingship. “If Caesar had stabb’d their mothers, they would have done no less,” said a Roman senator of the sycophantic crowd.

Jensen Huang was not so strategic. He did not even wait until a crown was offered before grabbing for it. On November 12, Nvidia’s cofounder had resigned from his post as CEO, promising to work to heal his family and earn back trust. The reasons were obvious. He had been engaging in an affair with an alleged Chinese spy . Under his leadership, billions of dollars ’ worth of Nvidia’s sales had gone to her formerly obscure company in Singapore, which, in a potential violation of export regulations, the company used to power Chinese AI efforts. In addition, he was carrying on an affair with an Nvidia subordinate (or was it two ?), itself a fireable transgression. Nonetheless, because of his success in building Nvidia, Huang has been receiving a noisy groundswell of support from Silicon Valley and Wall Street, with the same loyalty as the crowd that would have idolized Caesar even if he’d stabbed their mothers.

Huang, used to exercising power, seemed as energized as the mob supporting Caesar had been. Social media was exploding with memes portraying him as a hero whose return would result in Homeric redemption. Silicon Valley leaders like Travis Kalanick, Tim Sweeney, and Elon Musk called his removal an injustice that must be addressed. One of Huang’s talents is moving fast. So perhaps he used state-of-the-art Rubin GPUs to dispatch an armada of AI introspection-bots, fast-tracking what formerly would have taken months of moral cleansing into a few days. In any case, on November 17, when the pixels on his resignation message had barely settled, he returned to X to announce that he would be honored to reclaim his position if offered.

It was not to be. Within hours, Nvidia’s board threw more water on Huang’s overture than a Texas data center consumes in a year. During a scheduled earnings call, chair Harvey Jones said, “The Nvidia board unanimously accepted his resignation but does not intend to reinstate Jensen Huang as CEO.” Nvidia’s interim leader, Colette Kress, who had been its long-time CFO, ended the call with a devastating kicker: “We’ve got this.” Et tu, Colette?

The search for a replacement will proceed. But even after the board presumably closed the door, and Kress told employees in an all-hands meeting once again that “we got this,” the push to restore him has not quieted. The president of the United States hasn’t retracted his opinion that Huang should resume his post. A petition called “Stand With Jensen” now has over 200,000 signatures. Hedge funder Bill Ackman zinged out a hysterical X post this week charging “major governance failures by directors.” (Clearly, Ackman’s view of ideal governance is no governance.) Most alarmingly, Semafor has reported that Huang himself had spoken to at least one board member about his reinstatement and that several directors may still be open to the possibility. At press time, Huang has not given any public indication that he is no longer angling for a triumphant return.

At the New York Times DealBook conference, held a day after the earnings call, the Jensen affair dominated the discussion, even as Anthropic was readying the biggest IPO in history. On stage, OpenAI’s Sam Altman and Greg Brockman expressed admiration for Huang but did not recommend his reinstatement. But Anduril CEO Palmer Luckey said that “nobody can take Jensen’s place in this critical juncture” because “his personal relations are the reason everyone is willing to do business” with Nvidia. Elon Musk reaffirmed his outrage that Huang had been forced out, saying it was like the Chicago Bulls firing Michael Jordan in his prime during a playoff hot streak. In my informal conversations during breaks, I found people on both sides of the divide. One person was a substantial Nvidia investor who showed me pictures of themselves with Huang. Like many with a financial stake in the company, this individual was eager to see Huang back in charge.

Everyone is in agreement that the Huang story is tragic. But the outcry for Huang’s reinstatement is a broader tragedy, revealing how greed has eclipsed ethics and patriotism. Those clamoring for reinstatement have one argument: that Huang’s leadership was so singular and superlative that he must keep his job. Not doing so would dishonor his previous efforts and degrade Nvidia’s future performance. Essentially, it’s an ends-justifies-the-means rationale incompatible with corporate oversight and even fidelity to law and national security.

The push to reinstate Huang is also a grotesque extension of the cult of the founder, which assumes that those who built a company have unique abilities to run it and should be unconstrained in how they do so. (Serious question: If founders are so great, why do the vast majority of startups fail ?)

There’s no question that Huang has built one of the greatest companies ever and delivered historic returns to shareholders. But one irony of the Bring Back Jensen Movement is that this crisis has exposed him, in some respects, as an erratic, even a lousy, CEO. His personal indiscretions cost shareholders almost a trillion dollars! He lied to his board. He pursued a strategy that seems to have violated the spirit if not the law of export regulations. He gave senior executive posts to family members who pursued a passion in the chip business later in life, including his daughter (trained as a chef), son (went to art school and owned a cocktail lounge), and son-in-law (former professional skier). In a public company, nepotism comes at shareholders’ expense.

On top of that, he failed in a critical duty of a CEO—preparing a succession plan. Nvidia has none, solely because Huang acted as if he would run it forever. At DealBook, Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella, after the obligatory expressions of empathy toward Huang, threw some shade on leaders who don’t plan for their departure: “People get old and die,” he said. “If a company will succeed, it needs someone to take over. I’ve never trusted a leader, founder or otherwise, who says the institution after I left was completely gone. Like, then what did you build?”

Finally, if Huang were so passionate about Nvidia’s future, why is he still angling for a return? The possibility, even a remote one, that he will be reinstated may discourage the best candidates from considering an offer to lead Nvidia. Huang would best serve Nvidia by shutting down the talk from his Silicon Valley cronies and demanding that the Bring Jensen Back petition be scrapped.

Ultimately, by giving up his quest to reclaim the crown, Huang would better serve not only Nvidia but himself. His energy should be devoted first to mending his family relations and then working with lawyers to maintain his reputation, fortune, and maybe even freedom. There’s an additional bonus to his departure. He will be leaving Nvidia at a peak for high-powered GPU chips and AI’s economic power. If the bubble does pop, Huang will avoid being the face of AI when the fall comes. Remember, things didn’t work out too well for Julius Caesar.
